How to get out of a timeshare — without paying a scam exit company

If you own a timeshare you can't sell, and the maintenance fees keep coming year after year, you are not alone. A lot of owners feel trapped. The good news is that there are legitimate free or low-cost ways out of a timeshare, and you almost never have to pay thousands of dollars to a "timeshare exit company" to do it.

Your ways out, at a glance

There is no single button to press, but most owners have more than one path to explore. Start at the top of this list and work down. The cheapest, safest options come first.

  • The rescission (cooling-off) window. If you bought very recently, your contract and your state may give you a short period to cancel and get your money back. The exact number of days varies by state and by contract, so read your cancellation clause carefully and act fast.
  • A developer deed-back or take-back program. Many resorts and developers will take a timeshare back, sometimes at little or no cost. Ask the developer directly whether they have a "deed-back," "surrender," or "exit" program.
  • Resale or transfer. You may be able to sell, give away, or transfer the timeshare. Be realistic about price (see below) and watch out for resale scams that charge upfront fees.
  • A qualified attorney. If your contract is genuinely complicated, or you believe you were misled, a licensed attorney in your state can review your situation.
  • Only after that, a carefully vetted exit company. Some are legitimate, many are not. Treat this as a last resort, and research any company thoroughly before paying a cent.

Be careful with exit companies. The most common warning signs of a scam are large upfront fees, a "100% guarantee" that you'll get out, and high-pressure cold calls that push you to sign or pay right away. No one can honestly guarantee an exit. Before you pay or sign anything, look the company up with the Better Business Bureau (BBB) and check the FTC's consumer guidance at consumer.ftc.gov.

The honest truth about getting out

It helps to know what to expect. The resale value of most timeshares is very low, and often close to zero. That's hard to hear, but it's better to know it going in than to be surprised by a company that promises to "recover" money that was never really there.

At the same time, the situation is rarely hopeless. Developers do sometimes take timeshares back, especially from owners who are current on their fees and ask politely and persistently. Free and low-cost paths exist, and they are usually the first ones worth trying.

The single most expensive mistake owners make is rushing. When you feel trapped and a stranger calls promising a quick fix for a big fee, that pressure is exactly how people get scammed a second time — first by the sale, then by the "exit." Slow down. Read your paperwork. Verify anything you're told with your resort or developer, a licensed attorney, and the FTC before you hand over money.

Frequently asked questions

Can I really get out of a timeshare?

Often, yes, though it depends on your contract, your developer, and your state. Many owners find a way out through a deed-back program, a resale or transfer, or a rescission window if they bought recently. No one can promise a specific outcome, but for most people there is at least one honest path worth trying.

Is it free?

This site is completely free. Some ways out of a timeshare are also free or low-cost, such as asking your developer about a deed-back program or using your rescission window. Others may involve fees. We'll always point you toward the cheapest, safest options first.

Do I need to pay an exit company?

Usually not, and you should be cautious about doing so. Many owners resolve things directly with their developer or with help from a licensed attorney. If you do consider an exit company, treat it as a last resort and research it with the BBB and the FTC before paying anything.

How long do I have to cancel after buying?

It depends. Timeshare rescission (cooling-off) periods are set by state law and by your contract, and they vary. The window is often a few days up to around two weeks, but you should never rely on a single universal number. Read the cancellation clause in your own contract and check the rules for your state right away, because these windows are short and time-sensitive.
